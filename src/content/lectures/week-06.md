Five weeks in, you have four distinct analytical tools, built in a specific
order for a reason, and this screening's only job is to name them together
before you spend the set hour using all four on one artefact of your own
choosing. None of these lenses is optional for a strong Slop Teardown — a
teardown that only uses one of them reads as a list, not an argument.

## The tells (week 2): locate, don't gesture

"It looks fake" is not evidence; a frame number is. The habit week 2 built —
pointing at the specific second a hand gains a finger, or the exact clause
where a voiceover's cadence goes too smooth to be a read take — is the
teardown's evidentiary floor. Skip it and the rest of the argument has
nothing to stand on, no matter how sharp the economics or genre reading is.

## The direction (week 3): read the choices, or their absence

Prompting-as-direction gave you a way to read backward from a finished
artefact to the choices — or missing choices — that produced it. A teardown
that's paid attention here can say something sharper than "the continuity is
bad": it can say *which* directorial decision (an unspecified wardrobe
detail, an uncontrolled camera move) the continuity failure traces back to,
and what that implies about how much direction the artefact actually got
before it shipped.

## The economics (week 4): follow the money specifically

Not "this was probably cheap to make" — a cost-per-output figure, set
against what the traditional version would have cost, with a name for who
pockets the difference. This is the lens most drafts underuse, because it
requires guessing at numbers nobody will confirm; guess anyway, state the
assumption, and reason from it. An economic account that admits its numbers
are estimates is stronger than one that avoids numbers altogether.

## The genre (week 5): name what's being pastiched, and by whom

An artefact's genre conventions tell you what it's borrowing and what
audience-facing promise it's making — comfort-on-demand, credibility without
a falsifiable claim, urgency about history that was never checked. Naming
the genre precisely is also how you name the audience: a teardown that
identifies the sub-genre correctly has usually already answered "who is
this for and what is it built to make them feel," which is half of what a
strong analysis needs to do.

## Why all four, together

Each lens on its own describes a different part of the same object: what it
does (tells), how it was made (direction), why it was worth making that way
(economics), and what it's pretending to be (genre). A teardown that runs all
four against one artefact is making an argument about the object as a whole,
which is the difference between analysis and inventory — and the only thing
this course asks you to build without also asking you to build the thing
you're diagnosing.
