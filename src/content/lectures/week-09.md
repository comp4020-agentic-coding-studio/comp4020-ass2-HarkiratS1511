This is the screening the last two weeks were building toward, and it has one
argument: slop is a genre defined by its economics before it's defined by its
aesthetics, and the economics only becomes visible at the batch level, not
the clip level.

## Working the numbers

Take Halcyon's numbers, roughly, as a worked case rather than a verified
financial report — this course has no access to the ledger, and no company
confirms per-render cost anyway, which is itself worth noticing. Assume a
render costs on the order of forty cents of rented GPU time for an
eight-second clip, that Halcyon's cull rule keeps one render in twelve, and
that Halcyon posts around eighty finished shorts a day across four accounts.
That's roughly a thousand renders a day to get eighty survivors, on the order
of four hundred dollars in generation cost.

:::tip[The break-even line]
At the ad-revenue-share rates creators actually report for short-form
video — commonly quoted in low cents per thousand views, an order of
magnitude below what advertisers themselves pay for the inventory — those
eighty survivors need something like two to three million cumulative views a
day just to break even, before anyone's paid for their time.
:::

Halcyon's accounts each sit in the high hundred-thousands of followers. The
arithmetic works, but only just, and only
because generation cost keeps falling faster than ad rates do — which is the
actual economic engine of the genre: not that AI video is good enough yet,
but that it's cheap enough that "good enough" gets redefined downward every
quarter the render price drops.

## Why this explains the tells

Volume, not quality, is the correct unit of investment for a slop pipeline,
and this is why the genre's tells — the six-fingered hand, the caption font
that doesn't match the platform, the citation nobody checked — are
economically rational rather than accidental. Fixing them costs review time;
review time is the one line item that doesn't get cheaper with better
models, because it's human. A pipeline operator who spent an extra ninety
seconds per video on review would either need to review less often (worse
quality) or accept a worse cost-per-output than a competitor who doesn't
(worse margin). Slop's aesthetic signature is downstream of this constraint,
not upstream of it.

That's the argument the Slop Pipeline submission is asking you to make about
your own archetype: not "here is my automation," but "here is the cost
structure that makes this archetype worth automating, and here is what it
would take to make it not worth automating anymore." Bring your own numbers
to the work session; they don't need to be real, but they need to be
arguable.
