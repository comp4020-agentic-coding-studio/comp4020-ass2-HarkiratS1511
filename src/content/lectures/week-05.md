Pastiche in cinema is usually a deliberate authorial act: a director who
knows the slasher genre's rules well enough to quote them on purpose, trusting
the audience to recognise the quotation. Slop's genre pastiche is produced by
a different mechanism entirely — a model converging on whatever structural
pattern its training captions rewarded most consistently for a given prompt
category — and it has no awareness that it's quoting anything, no ironic
distance, no audience assumed to be in on it. Same structural echo, opposite
authorship. That difference is the actual argument this lecture is making,
not a claim that one version is more "genuine" than the other.

## Three sub-genres, three conventions each

**The AI history explainer.** A specific, recognisable shape: a cold-open
hook framed as a question ("what if the pyramids weren't built the way you
think"), narration that asserts rather than cites, and AI-generated imagery
standing in for footage or artefacts that were never photographed because
the events being described are exaggerated, decontextualised, or invented
outright. The convention isn't "it's about history" — plenty of real history
content shares that — it's the specific move of narrating uncertainty as
fact, because the model generating the script has no mechanism for flagging
its own confidence and no incentive, in this genre, to build one in.

**The product demo nobody filmed.** A cold open on the product already
mid-use, narration built entirely from feature-list phrasing ("engineered
for all-day comfort") rather than any specific claim a real user could
contradict, and a resolution that's always a direct offer, never a review.
Week 4's Everline Gadget Reviews specimen is this sub-genre exactly: nothing
in the video claims anything false, because nothing in the video claims
anything specific enough to be falsifiable. That vagueness is the load-bearing
convention, not a lazy shortcut around one.

**Faith-based inspirational content.** A devotional image or short — a
religious figure rendered in warm, oversaturated light, described as
"blessed" or offering comfort — captioned with scripture or a paraphrase of
it, and structured to end on a direct-address blessing or call to share.
The genre's actual audience-facing promise is comfort delivered on schedule,
which is precisely what a devotional-content pipeline can produce far more
reliably than a human congregation's actual pastoral care can, and precisely
why the genre generates such volume: comfort-on-demand is a real thing people
want, and a render farm can supply it every eleven minutes.

## Why "sincerity-agnostic" is the right term

A human pasticheur choosing to make a faith-based inspirational short on
purpose is making a claim about the genre — homage, satire, cynicism,
something. A model converging on the same structural beats because that
pattern scored best against its training objective for the prompt category
"inspirational devotional short" is making no claim at all. It cannot be
sincere about the content and cannot be ironic about it either, because both
require a stance the generation process doesn't have room for. What's left
is the genre's skeleton with nobody home — which is exactly why it's slop
rather than commentary on slop, no matter how precisely it hits every beat a
human pasticheur would also hit on purpose.

## What this sets up for week 6

Naming a genre's conventions from the inside — as this week's studio makes
you do, by trying to reproduce them — is the same skill the Slop Teardown
needs run in the other direction: given a finished artefact, name which
genre it's quoting, and whether anyone home decided to.
