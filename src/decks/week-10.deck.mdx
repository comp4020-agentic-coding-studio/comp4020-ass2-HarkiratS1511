# Sustained coherence

SLOP2758, week 10

---

## The claim

A single clip can look right by accident. Five linked clips that still look
like the same piece can't — that's the actual difficulty behind the
capstone, and it's a coherence problem, not a generation problem.

---

## Case study: Nightshade & Nectar

A supernatural-romance slop serial, posted in twice-weekly two-minute
instalments. Episode one establishes its lead: amber-lit, a locked reference
face, a recurring line delivered in the same too-smooth cadence every time.

It's a useful failure because it fails slowly.

---

## By episode nine

The lead's face has drifted enough that long-time viewers comment on it
before the plot does.

Nobody changed the seed on purpose. Seed-locking degrades under small prompt
edits, upstream model updates, and the ordinary entropy of a pipeline run by
different operators on different days.

---

{/* _class: banner */}

## Call this seed drift

---

## Seed drift is the six-fingered hand of sustained coherence

Not a single bad frame — a slow divergence a single-clip critique would
never catch, because a single-clip critique only ever sees one clip.

---

## Where coherence actually lives

Coherence lives in the parts of a production that don't show up in any one
frame:

- a locked colour grade, applied as a fixed post-process rather than
  described fresh in every prompt
- a reference-image set fed to every generation rather than a text
  description re-interpreted each time
- a story bible stating the two or three rules everyone touching the piece
  has to obey, regardless of tool or operator

---

## Nightshade & Nectar's actual bible

As far as it's inferable from the output: "amber, slow zoom, and the line
'I've been waiting.'"

Three rules — thin enough that the ninth episode can drift a face and still
count as on-brand, because the bible never specified the face.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

## "Enforced by a document someone actually checks against, not by everyone involved sharing good taste"

---

## Slop already borrows this from television

Conventional serial TV solved the same problem decades before anyone
automated it — with style guides, standing sets, and casting-continuity
clauses.

---

## The difference is what failure sounds like

- A slop pipeline's failure is silent — the seed drifts, and nobody notices
  until a viewer says something.
- A human production's failure is loud — an actor is visibly unavailable, a
  set burns down.

Sustained coherence, in either case, is enforced by a document. Not by taste.

## This week's set

Write that document for your own capstone, before the shooting — such as it
is — starts: a fixed colour or lighting rule, a method for keeping a
character or setting consistent, one recurring motif.

Then prove it on two test clips, made minutes or days apart, before week 11
asks your director's statement to account for what the bible commits you to.
