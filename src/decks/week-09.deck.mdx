# The business case for slop

SLOP2758, week 9

---

## The claim

Slop is a genre defined by its economics before it's defined by its
aesthetics. Weeks 7 and 8 built the pipeline mechanics. This is the argument
those mechanics were for.

---

## Where the economics lives

The economics of a slop pipeline is invisible at the clip level. One video
tells you nothing about whether the operation makes money. It only becomes
visible at the batch level — a day's renders, a day's survivors, a day's
views.

---

{/* _class: banner */}

## A worked case, not a verified financial report

---

## Halcyon's numbers

No company confirms per-render cost, and this course has no access to the
ledger. So: take Halcyon's numbers roughly, as a worked case. That
gap — nobody publishing the real figure — is itself worth noticing.

---

## The render

A render costs on the order of forty cents of rented GPU time, for one
eight-second clip.

---

## The cull

Halcyon's cull rule keeps one render in twelve. Eleven of every twelve
renders are thrown away before anyone sees them.

---

## The output

Around eighty finished shorts a day, across four accounts.

---

## What that costs

Eighty survivors, culled at one in twelve, is roughly a thousand renders a
day — on the order of four hundred dollars in generation cost.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

## Two to three million

At the ad-revenue-share rates creators actually report for short-form
video — commonly quoted in low cents per thousand views, an order of
magnitude below what advertisers pay for the inventory — those eighty
survivors need something like two to three million cumulative views a day
just to break even. Before anyone's paid for their time.

---

## Does it work

Halcyon's accounts each sit in the high hundred-thousands of followers. The
arithmetic works, but only just.

---

## Why it only just works

Not because AI video is good enough yet. Because generation cost keeps
falling faster than ad rates do. "Good enough" gets redefined downward every
quarter the render price drops. That's the actual engine, not the aesthetics.

---

{/* _class: banner */}

## Why this explains the tells

---

## Volume is the correct unit of investment

Not quality. The six-fingered hand, the caption font that doesn't match the
platform, the citation nobody checked — these are economically rational, not
accidental.

---

## Review time doesn't get cheaper

Fixing a tell costs review time. Review time is human, so it's the one line
item that doesn't fall with better models. Better models make rendering
cheaper. They don't make watching the output back any faster.

---

## The operator's choice

Spend an extra ninety seconds per video on review, and you either review
less often — worse quality — or accept a worse cost-per-output than a
competitor who doesn't. Slop's look is downstream of that constraint, not
upstream of it.

## What the Slop Pipeline submission asks for

Not "here is my automation." "Here is the cost structure that makes this
archetype worth automating, and here is what it would take to make it not
worth automating anymore."
